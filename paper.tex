\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We isolate five demonstration defects in a controlled 2D block-pushing task
while holding task, dataset size, and training protocol fixed. Across 640
policy fits, ten paired seeds, two policy families, and a transition-matched
control, defect cost is not determined by retained primary-expert action
information. At full contamination, accidental-success and occlusion data
have nearly equal label-fidelity MSE ($0.207$ and $0.212$), yet MLP rollout
success is $0.009$ and $0.866$; inconsistent-strategy data has worse label
fidelity ($0.475$) but higher success ($0.229$). Thus the robust result is not
merely that low-fidelity labels are harmful: accidental success exhibits a
late cliff, while the middle ranking changes with policy class. Offline
evaluation is similarly non-identifying: 49 policies with open-loop MSE in
$[0.20,0.30]$ span success from $0.130$ to $0.995$. The alternate controller
is exactly Markovian in the recorded 10-D state and its state-only oracle
succeeds on every rollout; nevertheless, its MLP clone attains low held-out
one-step error ($0.0568$) but only $0.229$ success. This separates observation
adequacy from closed-loop clonability and supports defect- and
learner-specific quality control rather than a universal data-quality score.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Behaviour cloning is a standard baseline for robot manipulation
\cite{pomerleau1989alvinn}, and its central known weakness is compounding
error: small action errors move the policy off the demonstration distribution,
where its predictions degrade further \cite{ross2011dagger}. The standard
responses to this are architectural or algorithmic: better policy classes
\cite{chi2023diffusion}, or interactive data collection.

Demonstrations fail in structured ways: a camera is occluded, an operator
fumbles into success, strategies disagree, or an episode ends early. These
defects differ in whether states, actions, temporal coverage, or strategy are
altered. A finite quality-control budget therefore raises a narrower question
than whether ``better data helps'': after accounting for retained expert
action information, do defect type and learner still change the outcome?

\subsection{Related work and scope}

RoboMimic established that offline human-data quality and the mismatch between
training and rollout objectives materially affect robot-learning conclusions
\cite{mandlekar2021what}. Belkhale et al. separately study data quality in
imitation learning \cite{belkhale2023data}. Methods for imperfect or
suboptimal demonstrations commonly estimate confidence or importance weights
\cite{wu2019imperfect,wang2021weight}, or learn discriminator-derived BC
weights \cite{xu2022dwbc}. Other work critiques and filters heterogeneous
demonstrations \cite{kuhar2023discern} or retrieves relevant behaviours from
larger datasets \cite{du2023retrieval}. These methods ask how to learn despite
mixed-quality data. Our smaller contribution is diagnostic: compare named
pipeline defects at controlled prevalence, measure their primary-expert label
fidelity directly, and test whether offline action error identifies the
resulting closed-loop damage. RoboMimic's observation that training and
rollout objectives can select different checkpoints motivates, rather than
conflicts with, this controlled comparison \cite{mandlekar2021what}.

This paper answers both questions in a deliberately small, fully controlled
setting. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item A controlled design treating demonstration quality as the independent
variable across five failure modes, ten paired seeds, and two policy families.
\item A label-fidelity control showing that full-contamination performance is
not monotone in disagreement with the primary expert.
\item A similar-offline-score comparison showing that open-loop MSE does not
uniquely identify closed-loop success; nested correlations are secondary.
\item Policy-, transition-, and state-only-oracle controls separating defect
effects, strategy mixing, observation adequacy, and clonability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method}

\subsection{Environment}

\textbf{Push2D} is a 2D block-pushing task implemented in vectorised NumPy. A
point gripper must move a block into a goal region. Pushing was chosen over
reaching deliberately: it imposes a \emph{staging} requirement, since the
gripper must position itself behind the block relative to the goal before
pushing is productive. This makes compounding error consequential. A policy
that drifts during approach ends up on the wrong side and pushes the block
\emph{away} from the goal, which is unrecoverable within the episode budget.
This is precisely the class of failure that open-loop metrics are structurally
poor at surfacing, since each individual action error is small.

Observations are 10-dimensional (gripper, block and goal positions plus two
relative vectors); actions are 2D displacements clipped to $[-1,1]$. Episodes
run for at most 110 steps and terminate on success, defined as the block
centre falling within $0.05$ of the goal.

\subsection{Scripted experts}

Using scripted rather than human demonstrations is what makes the experiment
controlled: corruption can be applied with exact, programmatic ground truth
rather than human judgement about what counts as a bad demo.

The \textbf{primary expert} is a hand-coded orbit-then-push controller that
circles the block at a safe radius until aligned, then pushes toward the goal.
Measured success rate: $\mathbf{100.0\%}$.

The \textbf{alternate expert} always circles in the same rotational direction
from a wider staging radius, producing a visibly different route through state
space. It was explicitly tuned until it \emph{also} reaches
$\mathbf{100.0\%}$, so that mixing the two tests strategy \emph{inconsistency}
rather than strategy \emph{quality}. This tuning turns out to matter
substantially (Section~\ref{sec:controls}). Importantly, the controller is a
deterministic function of the current 10-D observation; it has no hidden
memory or history input.

The \textbf{naive controller} charges blindly at the block and never reasons
about the goal; the block travels along whatever ray the gripper started on.
It succeeds on approximately 6\% of episodes, purely by geometric luck.

\subsection{Failure modes}

Each mode produces demonstrations that a real pipeline could plausibly log:

\textbf{Occlusion.} A contiguous window covering 35\% of timesteps has its
observations frozen at the last visible frame. Actions remain
expert-quality; the demonstrator can see, the camera cannot. The resulting
transitions have corrupted inputs and pristine labels.

\textbf{Accidental success.} Rollouts from the naive controller, keeping only
those that reached the goal. Correct outcome, no correct behaviour anywhere in
the episode.

\textbf{Corrective flailing.} Heavy uniform jitter on the first 12 timesteps,
after which the demonstrator settles into correct motion and still succeeds.

\textbf{Truncation.} A clean episode cut at 70\% of its length and logged
anyway, what a QC filter missing a premature stop looks like.

\textbf{Inconsistent strategy.} Complete episodes from the alternate expert.
Every one is a genuine success; the only defect is disagreement with the
primary strategy about what to do in a given state.

\subsection{Two design decisions}

\textbf{Action-level corruptions are re-simulated, not pasted.} Overwriting
actions in a logged trajectory without re-running the dynamics yields
observations that no longer follow from the actions, a physically impossible
episode. That measures label noise, not bad demonstrations. Every mode that
touches actions replays the episode through the environment.

\textbf{Corrupted episodes must pass the same success-based QC filter a real
pipeline applies.} Modes are rejection-sampled to successful episodes only.
Truncation is the deliberate exception, since it \emph{is} the filter failing.

\subsection{Sweep, policies, evaluation and uncertainty}

The sweep axis is \textbf{contamination rate} $\rho$, not per-episode
severity. Every dataset contains the same number of episodes; $\rho$ of them
are defective and $(1-\rho)$ are clean, with per-episode severity held fixed.
This places all five modes on one comparable axis and answers the question a
pipeline actually asks: what fraction of the dataset can be this kind of bad
before the policy breaks?

We sweep $\rho \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0\}$ across ten training
seeds. The shared clean baseline is fitted once per seed, producing 260 fits
per policy family: ten clean fits and $5\times5\times10$ contaminated fits.
The primary policy is a $2\times256$ ReLU MLP trained by MSE behaviour cloning
for 50 epochs with Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 256, and no
weight decay. A robustness sweep uses an Extra Trees regressor with 40 trees,
all input features available at each split, and a minimum leaf size of two
\cite{geurts2006extratrees}. Both train on the same 400-episode datasets.
Because episode length varies, these contain 5,957--19,180 transitions across
the sweep (clean datasets average 12,207); the transition-matched control below
removes this variation.

Because alternate-strategy episodes are longer, we add a control that fixes
the dataset at 13,000 transitions and defines $\rho$ as the exact fraction of
transitions drawn from that strategy. Repeating all six mixture levels for
both policy families and ten seeds adds 120 fits, for 640 fits in total.

Two metrics are recorded per policy. \textbf{Open-loop MSE} is
action-prediction error against 300 held-out \emph{clean} expert episodes.
\textbf{Closed-loop success rate} runs the policy over 200 episodes. Evaluation
seed $12345+s$ is used for training seed $s$: evaluation states vary across
seeds but are paired across configurations within a seed. We report means and
two-sided 95\% Student-t intervals across the ten paired seeds;
full-contamination changes are paired against the corresponding clean fit.

Two diagnostics address alternative explanations. \textbf{Label fidelity} is
the MSE between each logged demonstration action and the primary expert's
action evaluated on that same logged observation, summarized over ten
independently generated full-contamination datasets per mode. This quantity
measures retained primary-expert action information, not trajectory success.
For the alternate strategy, we also evaluate cloned-policy MSE on 300 held-out
alternate-expert episodes and compare its rollout success with the original
state-only alternate controller on the same evaluation seeds.

\section{Results}

The MLP clean baseline reaches $0.949\pm0.015$ closed-loop success (mean and
95\% t-interval half-width); the Extra Trees baseline reaches
$0.996\pm0.003$.

\subsection{The ranking is not explained by label fidelity}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the headline comparison. At full contamination,
corrective flailing changes MLP mean success by $+0.016\pm0.022$ relative to
the paired clean baseline, whereas accidental success changes it by
$-0.940\pm0.020$. Both are ``bad
demonstrations'' under a practical taxonomy, yet their observed costs differ
sharply.

That contrast alone could be tautological because flailing retains mostly
expert actions whereas accidental success does not. The explicit fidelity
control rejects a one-dimensional explanation. Accidental-success and
occlusion labels have nearly identical disagreement with the primary expert
($0.207$ versus $0.212$ MSE), but their full-contamination success differs by
$0.857$. Inconsistent-strategy labels disagree most ($0.475$) yet clone far
better than accidental-success data. Truncation has essentially perfect label
fidelity but is benign for the MLP and damaging for Extra Trees
(Section~\ref{sec:controls}). Performance is therefore not monotone in retained
primary-expert action information. The narrower robust claim is the accidental
success cliff and a policy-dependent middle ranking.

\textbf{Corrective flailing causes little degradation in this setting.}
Jitter-then-recover trajectories may provide DAgger-like coverage of
off-distribution states \cite{ross2011dagger}; at $\rho=1.0$, MLP mean success
is $0.965\pm0.015$. The paired interval includes zero change, so these data do
not establish a benefit, but they make flailing a lower QC priority here.

\textbf{Truncation does not reduce mean success in the observed setting}:
MLP mean success is $0.962\pm0.017$ at $\rho=1.0$. A plausible explanation is that the push action can be
extrapolated from the retained approach phase. This interpretation should not
be transferred to tasks with a distinct terminal phase, such as grasping,
without further experiments.

\subsection{Accidental success is a cliff, not a slope}

The most operationally significant observed result is the shape of the accidental
success curve: $0.939$ at $\rho=0.5$, $0.917$ at $\rho=0.75$, $0.666$ at
$\rho=0.9$, and $\mathbf{0.009}$ at $\rho=1.0$.

In this experiment, a minority of clean demonstrations masks much of the
problem. A pipeline monitoring only success-labelled demonstration throughput
would not reveal the approaching policy failure. The
diagnostic wrong-side push rate tells the same story, rising from $0.011$ at
$\rho=0.5$ to $0.415$ at $\rho=1.0$: the policy has learned to shove the block
in essentially arbitrary directions.

\subsection{Open-loop evaluation has failure-mode-dependent value}

The primary evidence is a similar-score comparison: among the 49 runs whose
open-loop MSE lies in the descriptive band $[0.20,0.30]$, closed-loop success
ranges from $\mathbf{0.130}$ to $\mathbf{0.995}$. The band was selected after
inspection and is not a statistical equivalence test; it demonstrates
non-identification---numerically similar offline scores can accompany nearly
the full range of task outcomes. Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} shows the complete
distribution.

Correlations provide secondary context. Pooled across all 260 distinct MLP
fits, open-loop MSE correlates with success at $r=-0.92$, but this aggregate is
driven partly by between-mode separation and high-MSE collapse points.
Within-mode coefficients range from $-0.13$ to $-0.95$. Table~\ref{tab:range}
makes the associated range restriction explicit: truncation and flailing have
success SDs near $0.03$, so their weak coefficients are not evidence that MSE
would fail over a wider outcome range. Each coefficient is descriptive over
60 observations nested within ten seeds and six contamination levels; the
points are not independent draws and no correlation significance test is
claimed.

\section{Robustness controls}
\label{sec:controls}

\subsection{Policy family changes the ranking}

Table~\ref{tab:policies} repeats the full-contamination comparison with Extra
Trees. Accidental success remains catastrophic and corrective flailing remains
benign. The middle of the ranking changes substantially: trees are robust to
stale observations but sensitive to truncation, whereas the MLP shows the
opposite pattern. Demonstration defects therefore interact with policy
inductive bias; only the extreme cases are stable enough here to support a
cross-policy priority.

\subsection{Strategy mixing and observation adequacy}

We initially predicted that mixing two valid strategies would cause
multimodal-averaging collapse. The endpoint control contradicts that reading:
at $\rho=1.0$ the dataset is fully self-consistent, so its low success is a
\emph{clonability floor}, not evidence of mixing harm. Under episode matching,
the MLP's observed success exceeds the line between its $\rho=0$ and $\rho=1$
endpoints at every intermediate mixture.

The stricter transition-matched control fixes both total transitions and the
alternate-strategy transition fraction. Its intermediate MLP means are
$0.962$, $0.966$, $0.741$, and $0.386$; Extra Trees means are $0.984$,
$0.888$, $0.797$, and $0.563$. Every point remains above the corresponding
endpoint interpolation. Across both matching schemes, these curves support
unequal clonability, rather than strategy conflict, as the dominant
explanation (Figure~\ref{fig:strategy-control}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{results/fig3_strategy_controls.png}
\caption{Episode-matched and transition-matched strategy controls. Points are
means and error bars are 95\% t intervals over ten paired seeds. Every

\label{fig:strategy-control}

The proposed hidden-history explanation for that floor does not hold here:
the alternate controller is implemented as a deterministic function of the
current 10-D observation. The state-only oracle succeeds on all 2,000
evaluation rollouts across the ten seeds. An MLP trained only on alternate
demonstrations also achieves held-out alternate-trajectory MSE
$0.0568\pm0.0041$, yet closed-loop success remains $0.229\pm0.102$ (95\%
t-interval half-widths). Observation is therefore sufficient for the scripted
expert, while one-step regression is not sufficient for a robust clone. This
pattern is consistent with compounding error or sensitivity near the
controller's switching geometry, but the present diagnostic does not identify
which mechanism dominates.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Controlled simulation only.} Push2D omits 3D geometry, contact
uncertainty, visual perception, and hardware latency. The study tests a method
for isolating defect effects, not transferable numerical thresholds.

\textbf{Two deterministic regressors.} The MLP and Extra Trees differ in
inductive bias, but both return point actions. Multimodal generative policies,
including diffusion policies \cite{chi2023diffusion}, may respond differently
to inconsistent strategies.

\textbf{Finite seed and evaluation coverage.} Ten paired seeds materially
improve precision, and evaluation states vary by seed, but each fit is still
tested on only 200 initial conditions from the same simulator distribution.

\textbf{Exploratory analysis.} The failure modes and MSE band were not
preregistered, and intervals are not adjusted for multiple comparisons. They
quantify run-to-run uncertainty rather than establish universal effects.

\textbf{Scripted demonstrations.} Programmatic defects provide exact control
but do not capture the full diversity or temporal dependence of human
data-collection errors.

\section{Conclusion}

Treating demonstration quality as the independent variable reveals both
stable and policy-dependent effects that cannot be reduced to action-label
fidelity. Accidental-success and occlusion demonstrations disagree similarly
with the primary expert but produce radically different MLP policies;
occlusion and truncation also exchange rank across the MLP and Extra Trees.
The defensible result is therefore narrower than a universal hierarchy:
accidental success has a late cliff, while QC priority in the middle depends
on defect structure and the intended learner.

The similar-MSE band is the main evaluation result: nearly equal offline
scores coexist with success from $0.130$ to $0.995$. Correlations are only
descriptive because seed and contamination observations are nested and some
modes have restricted outcome ranges. Finally, transition matching and a
state-only oracle turn an apparent strategy-conflict or observation-aliasing
effect into a clonability result. Offline screening remains useful, but it
does not substitute for closed-loop validation.
